Weight tying and token embeddings

Weight Tying Biases Token Embeddings Towards the Unembedding Space

A. Lopardo, A. Harish, C. Arnett, A. Gupta

ACL Findings 2026

Many language models use weight tying: the matrix that turns tokens into input vectors and the matrix that turns the final hidden state into next-token scores are the same matrix. Tying saves parameters, but many recent large models have gone back to separate matrices. We asked what tying does to the embedding space, and why it seems to hurt at scale.

A tied matrix has two jobs that pull in different directions: represent tokens for the early layers, and predict the next token at the end. We compared tied and untied models, including a matched pair of OLMo-1B runs and the GPT-Neo, Pythia, and Qwen3 families, by aligning their embedding spaces with identity, orthogonal, and linear maps and by comparing nearest neighbors and spectra. The tied matrix looks much more like an untied output matrix than an untied input embedding, so it is shaped mainly for output prediction.

The reason shows up in training. Early on, the gradient from the output side dominates the gradient from the input side, so the shared matrix drifts toward the output role. A tuned lens analysis shows the cost: the early layers of tied models are less compatible with the final output space and contribute less to the residual stream. Scaling up the input-side gradients during training pulls the tied matrix back toward input-like structure, which gives causal evidence for the gradient imbalance. It does not improve downstream performance, though, so simple rescaling is not a free fix.

The takeaway is that tying trades input representation for output prediction, which may explain why it hurts at scale. For large models, where the parameter savings are small, untying is worth it. For small models, where embeddings are a large share of the parameters, tying still saves a lot, and better training interventions could keep those savings while reducing the cost.

A brain-routed mixture-of-blocks language model

Berkeley AI Researchwith Meta FAIRPaper in preparation

Mixture-of-experts models send tokens to different components, but nothing guarantees those components specialize in something a person could interpret. This project asks whether the brain can supply that structure.

We split a pretrained language model into blocks and route between them using clusters of fMRI voxels, so each block corresponds to a group of brain responses. We then test two things: whether a router that only sees text can recover the brain's routing, and whether the blocks stay specialized after fine-tuning. For the second, I developed a mutual-information routing objective that restores the specialization that supervised fine-tuning otherwise erodes.

Cross-lingual fairness in LLM moral reasoning

Cross-lingual Fairness Drift in LLM Moral Reasoning

E. Xie, A. Chang-Lee, A. Harish, A. Vaidheeswaran

ICLR CAO Workshop 2026

A behavioral study of how language models reason about moral dilemmas in English, Spanish, Korean, and Mandarin. We score responses on a multi-part rubric and measure how much the models' reasoning shifts across languages, which makes language a useful axis for fairness audits.
